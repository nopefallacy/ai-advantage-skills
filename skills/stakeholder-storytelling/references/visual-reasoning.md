# Choose visuals by the relationship they explain

Use these as starting points for judgment, not fixed psychological rules.

| Stakeholder question | Useful visual forms | Meaning to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where is work getting stuck? | Annotated process, queue, dependency chain, constrained passage | The actual constraint and its upstream or downstream consequences |
| What changes under the proposal? | Matched before/after views, state transition, aligned comparison | Comparable scope and a clearly identified change |
| How do many parts fit together? | System map, grouped nodes, layered structure | Real relationships, boundaries, and dependencies |
| Which choice fits our priorities? | Comparison matrix, trade-off plot, ranked alternatives | Criteria, assumptions, and the costs of each option |
| What is changing over time? | Line chart, small multiples, milestone sequence | Actual temporal order, units, and appropriate baselines |
| Where does the difference come from? | Contribution chart, decomposition, waterfall when mathematically appropriate | Components reconcile to the stated total |
| How widespread is this? | Distribution, coverage matrix, geographic map only when location matters | Denominator, population, and missing coverage |
| What happens if we change an assumption? | Scenario comparison or interactive sensitivity view | Assumptions remain visible and calculated outputs are distinguished from observed facts |

## Make the metaphor earn its place

Translate the idea into relationships before selecting the metaphor: accumulation, friction, connection, dependency, imbalance, concentration, or change. Check that the proposed picture preserves the important relationship and does not introduce a false one.

A narrow passage may explain a verified capacity constraint. It does not establish that a particular team is responsible for delay. A bridge may explain a needed connection; it cannot establish that the connection will succeed. A chess piece is not automatically a useful illustration for every strategy discussion.

Pair unfamiliar metaphors with direct labels. If the viewer needs a long explanation of the metaphor before understanding the work, simplify it or choose a direct diagram.

## Present the numbers as evidence

Use supplied or verified data. If the user asks for an illustrative scenario, identify it as illustrative and keep invented values separate from actual results. Do not manufacture improvements to make a before/after visual compelling.

Make units, date ranges, denominators, definitions, and material assumptions available where they affect interpretation. Use comparisons with compatible scope. Do not imply causation from a correlation or let a diagram conceal uncertainty.

For dashboards, decide what question each view answers. A total without its period, a percentage without its denominator, or a status without its definition may look complete while failing to inform a decision. Filters should change the relevant views coherently and show their current scope. Static data should not be presented as live.

## Write around the visual

Prefer a meaningful sentence over a generic heading such as "Overview" when the evidence supports a conclusion. Match the certainty of that sentence to the source material. Use short, descriptive labels and enough connected prose for a reader to follow the reasoning.

Remove em dashes from generated stakeholder-facing copy without mechanically replacing them with awkward punctuation. Read the wording for natural phrasing, unnecessary abstraction, and unexplained terminology. Respect exact quoted source material; paraphrase outside quotation marks if its punctuation does not fit the requested style.
